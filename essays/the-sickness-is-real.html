The Sickness Is Real, the Prescription Is More of It

Chad Crowley, writing at Riding the Tiger, has produced an essay that takes Oswald Spengler seriously and follows him where he leads. It diagnoses, with more precision than most of our contemporaries can manage, the condition we are in. Institutions hollowed out. Form persisting without animating force. A civilization governed by abstractions severed from their source, by money that has ceased to be an instrument and become the thing itself. A culture in its Zivilisation phase, running on stored momentum while the creative spring that once fed it has gone dry.

All of this, I grant. Grant it fully. The diagnosis is not wrong. The hollowing is real. The exhaustion is visible. Anyone with eyes to see can see it.

What follows in Crowley's essay has the surface form of exposition. But by the closing paragraph, the Spenglerian framing has fallen away and the author is speaking in his own voice about the present moment: this is the age now unfolding before us... authority gathers in the figure of the great man... blood stands not as metaphor but as reality... what comes next is not decided by argument, nor by principle, but by power. This is no longer summary. This is endorsement in the indicative mood, presented as fact about the world rather than as a position one might hold or refuse.

The move he is making is this: principles no longer command, only power remains, and what is coming is the return of rule grounded in naked force. Caesarism. The concentration of authority in the figure of the great man whose will becomes the organizing center of political life. And beneath that, as the elemental reality toward which everything tends, blood — not as metaphor, but as the primordial fact that asserts itself when abstraction has exhausted itself. A power can be overthrown only by another power and not by a principle. What comes next is decided not by argument but by strength.

This is wrong. Not because the diagnosis is wrong. Not because the trajectory is implausible. Because the prescription is more of the sickness — and because telling people the sickness is the cure is its own act of defection from the work that might actually heal them.

Consider what Spengler's argument actually rests on. Strip away the erudition and the cadence and what remains is a metaphor. Cultures are like organisms. Organisms are born, mature, decline, die. Therefore cultures are born, mature, decline, die. The organism analogy does all the work. It is what lets him call the terminal phase destined, what lets him treat Caesarism as the necessary expression of an age rather than a choice, what allows the whole framework to foreclose agency in the name of rigor. If you grant the analogy, the fatalism follows. If you question it, the fatalism collapses.

So let us question it.

In November of 2025, two researchers — Martin Picard at Columbia and Nirosha Murugan at Wilfrid Laurier — published a paper in Cell Metabolism that almost no one outside a narrow circle has read. They call their framework the Energy Resistance Principle. The claim, stated simply: living organisms are energetic systems that transform food-derived electrons into useful work through the resistance of biological circuitry. Some resistance is necessary — without it, no transformation is possible. But excess resistance produces exactly what we have always called the hallmarks of aging and disease: oxidative stress, inflammation, molecular damage, information loss. The paper argues, with empirical grounding, that these hallmarks are not the inevitable expression of a biological life cycle but the cumulative signature of a regulatory failure — an organism that has lost the capacity to manage energy within its required limits.

The Picard and Murugan framework is not the only serious account of aging in the contemporary literature, and I do not pretend to adjudicate among them here. What matters for the present argument is more modest. The view that aging is the inevitable expression of an organic life cycle — the view that for two centuries lent its certainty to every analogy that wanted to claim civilizational inevitability — is no longer the only serious view. There is now a body of credentialed work, of which Picard and Murugan are one strong example, arguing that what we have called aging is the signature of regulatory failure rather than the unfolding of biological destiny.

Aging, in this view, is not what organisms do because that is what organisms do. Aging is what happens when the regulation fails.

Read that again, because it is the hinge of this essay.

For two hundred years, the organism has been the metaphor of choice for anyone wishing to lend philosophical weight to the claim that some process is inevitable. Cultures are organisms. Empires are organisms. Institutions are organisms. The metaphor works because we all knew, as a kind of pre-theoretical certainty, that organisms age and die. It was the one fixed point to which the analogy could anchor. If the source of the metaphor is revealed to be not a law of nature but a regulatory failure — if the thing we thought was destiny turns out to be dysregulation — then every argument built on the analogy loses its floor.

Spengler's civilization does not have to age. The organism he reasoned from does not have to age. The whole edifice was resting on a biology that was not quite biology.

So what is the civilization actually doing, if not expressing an organic life cycle?

It is doing what any cooperative order does when it stops regulating its defectors.

Cooperation is not a natural resting state. It is a costly, fragile, hard-won equilibrium that exists only where defection is reliably punished — where someone, at some price to themselves, enforces the norm against the one who takes the benefits of the order without paying its costs. Law does this. Honor does this. Religion does this. Social shame does this. Violence, sometimes, does this. A civilization is a cooperative order at vast scale, and it persists exactly as long as it retains the will and capacity to police its own perimeter against those who would extract from it without contributing.

What Spengler describes as senescence is, I would submit, the predictable equilibrium of a cooperative order that has outsourced its defector-excision function to institutions which have themselves been captured by defectors. The dictatorship of money he names so clearly is not the inevitable late phase of Faustian civilization. It is what happens when a particular class of extractors has successfully disarmed the mechanisms that would have held them accountable, and has trained the remaining cooperators to regard enforcement itself as barbaric. The hollowing is real, but it is not destiny. It is the accumulated cost of unpunished defection.

This frame must be handled carefully. A defector is not a stranger, not a dissenter, not a person with unfamiliar views. A defector is specifically someone who takes the benefits of cooperation while refusing its costs. The hollowing was not done to us by strangers. It was done largely by people who still, today, consider themselves pillars of the order they were dismantling.

Which is why the Spenglerian prescription is more of the sickness. Caesarism does not restore regulation. It replaces impersonal defection with personal defection. It does not re-enforce the cooperative norm; it suspends it in favor of the will of the man. The cooperator is bound — to the inheritance, to the covenant, to the standard above himself that judges his use of power. Caesar is bound to nothing. That is the whole of his appeal and the whole of his failure. Every civilization has been offered this bargain at its hour of exhaustion, and the ones that accepted it did not recover. They stabilized for a season, as Spengler admits, and then the season ended. Power without fidelity is just the next turning of the wheel he describes — concentration, exhaustion, concentration again. Blood without law is not the return of the organic; it is the final abandonment of the thing that made the civilization worth defending.

There is another option, and there has always been another option, and I want to name it by the name it deserves.

In the winter of 878, Alfred of Wessex was hiding in a marsh. The Danes had overrun everything. His army was scattered, his kingdom lost, the old Saxon order a ruin. By every Spenglerian metric, this was the terminal phase — a culture conquered from without and hollowed from within, abandoned by its nobility, reduced to a king in a swamp. The prescription, had Spengler been there, would have been clear: power has withdrawn from abstraction and gathers once more in living will. What comes next is decided not by principle but by strength.

Alfred did something else.

He came out of the marsh. He won at Edington. The conventional move, the move every chronicler would have understood, was to put Guthrum to the sword.

He did not execute Guthrum. He stood as godfather at the Dane's baptism. He translated Boethius from Latin into English with his own hand, sitting at a desk in a kingdom that by any reasonable measure should not have existed. He built a legal code that bound the king as it bound the ploughman. He founded schools. He established the burh system — not as the architecture of personal dominance, but as a distributed capacity for cooperative defense. He did, in short, the slow and costly work of rebuilding regulation — of restoring the cooperative order by insisting, against every available incentive to do otherwise, that the king was also under the law.

Alfred was a Caesar who knelt. He walked into the moment that Spengler would have described as the arrival of naked power, and he declined the offer. He held something above himself — the inheritance, the covenant, the God who stood over the king as the king stood over the realm — and because he held it, the thing he was handed was not merely preserved. It was handed forward.

Spengler's framework has no category for Alfred. It can only see power, and Alfred was something else.

Alfred was the cooperator at the hour when cooperation was asked to bear its full cost. Call it what it is: fidelity. The bond of the powerful to the standard above themselves — the thing Caesar refuses, and the thing Alfred kept.

✱

That is the move this age requires of us. Not Caesarism. Not the welcoming of the great man who will suspend the hollowed forms in favor of his own will. Not the cynic's counsel to position oneself for what comes after. This age requires the costly work — the quiet, unglamorous, expensive work of being a cooperator in a late order; of honoring the inheritance; of excising the defection one encounters, beginning with the defection one is tempted to commit oneself; of translating the books that must be translated; of standing godfather to the enemy one has defeated; of building law in a swamp when by every reasonable standard no kingdom should exist.

The life cycle as a fixed point is gone. The certainty that the organism must age has loosened, and with it the analogy that civilizations must die. What was called destiny may be dysregulation, and dysregulation is addressable. Not easily. Not cheaply. Not by argument alone. But by the people who decide, in the marsh, that they will build the thing anyway.

I will not mistake the sickness for the cure. I will not be told that the exhaustion of the forms means the exhaustion of the forms' purpose. I will not accept the counsel of those who, having accurately named the hollowing, now invite us to fill the hollow with blood.

There is a king at a desk in Wessex. The kingdom does not yet exist. He is translating a Roman philosopher into the language of a people who do not yet know they are a people. Outside, the marsh. Inside, the candle, the manuscript, the slow work of regulation, the refusal to go quietly into the silence that the age says is coming.

We have been here before. Someone held it. Someone can hold it again.

Let us ride.
